## Q5.5

> Explain what the following code does: ((n & (n-1)) == 0).

`n=1` -- `(0b1 & 0b0) == 0b0`. True.
`n=2` -- `(0b10 & 0b01) == 0b00`. True.
`n=3` -- `(0b11 & 0b10) == 0b10`. False.
`n=4` -- `(0b100 & 0b011) == 0b000`. True.
`n=5` -- `(0b101 & 0b100) == 0b100`. False.
`n=6` -- `(0b110 & 0b101) == 0b100`. False.
`n=7` -- `(0b111 & 0b110) == 0b110`. False.
`n=8` -- `(0b1000 & 0b0111) == 0b000`. True.

So it appears to be "Is this a power of 2?" Why would that formula tell us whether or not it's a power of 2?

### If it **is** a power of 2:

Powers of 2 have their left-most digit as 1, and the rest as 0s. So subtracting 1 will give us the left-most digit as 0, and the rest as 1s. Bitwise-and will always give us 0b0.

### If it's **not** a power of 2:

The unifying idea for both cases below: **`n - 1` clears the lowest set bit and leaves every bit above it unchanged.** Since a non-power-of-2 has at least one set bit *above* the lowest one, and those upper bits are identical in `n` and `n - 1`, the AND preserves that upper `1` — so `n & (n-1) != 0`. The odd/even split below is just "how far the borrow travels."

#### If it's odd:

If it's odd, then `n - 1` just changes the lowest bit (a 1) from 1 to 0; every higher bit is untouched. Because `n` isn't a power of 2, there is at least one other `1` up there, and it's unchanged in both `n` and `n - 1`, so it survives the AND. Result is non-zero.

#### If it's even:

There are 1 or more 0's at the bottom of the number. In regex (the leading group must contain at least one `1`, or it would be a power of 2):

`n = ([01]*1[01]*)1([0])+`

The trailing 0's will become 1's. The 1 directly above them will become a 0. The rest of the number (the leading group) remains unchanged. Because that leading group is **identical** in `n` and `n - 1` and contains at least one `1`, the AND preserves that `1`, so `n & (n-1) != 0`. Result is non-zero.
